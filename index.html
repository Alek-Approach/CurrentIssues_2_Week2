Can breakfast help you handle stress?
Adapted for class from a Binghamton University news release, October 13, 2026

Do you feel stressed when something goes wrong? A new study suggests that simple daily habits may help. Eating breakfast, exercising, and sleeping well are connected to a mental skill called psychological flexibility [the ability to change how you think and act when a situation changes].

Researchers at Binghamton University in New York studied about 400 college students. The students answered an anonymous survey about their food, sleep, exercise, and other habits.

The study found that students with healthy habits usually had more psychological flexibility. They were also more resilient [able to recover from problems]. Here are the key results:

  • Students who ate breakfast at least five times a week were more resilient.
  • Students who slept less than six hours had lower flexibility and lower resilience.
  • Exercise for 20 minutes or more was linked to more flexibility.
  • Taking fish oil several times a week was also linked to more flexibility.

The opposite was also true. Students who often ate fast food or did not sleep enough found it harder to change their thoughts and behavior.

The lead researcher, Lina Begdache, gives an example. Imagine a person who misses a flight. Instead of panicking, a flexible person stays calm and adapts. “This person may still feel stressed, but they’re better able to manage it,” she said.

According to Begdache, flexible people can “step back” and think: “I feel this because of that. What can I do?” Naming your feelings can help you find a solution.

Begdache says the new finding is this: a healthy diet and lifestyle help people build flexibility, and flexibility then makes them more resilient.

However, the study used surveys. It shows a connection between habits and stress, but it does not prove that breakfast or sleep causes better stress control.

Three kinds of information
  • Main point = the one big idea of the whole article.
  • Key detail = an important fact that supports the main point (results, numbers).
  • Extra detail = interesting, but you don’t need it in a summary.

Diet, exercise, and sleep
Adapted for class from the Sleep Foundation, updated October 5, 2026

Introduction. Diet, exercise, and sleep are three “pillars” [strong supports] of a healthy life. They affect each other. Improving all three may be better for your body and mind than improving just one.

Card A
Diet

What you eat affects almost every part of your health. A healthy, balanced diet lowers the risk of heart disease, diabetes, and other conditions. Some studies suggest that certain diets may also lower the risk of depression and anxiety. Food affects sleep too: caffeine can make it hard to fall asleep, and eating too close to bedtime can disturb your sleep.

Card B
Exercise

Exercise helps almost every system in the body. Some benefits come quickly, like less anxiety and better sleep. Over time, exercise also makes bones stronger and lowers the risk of disease. Running and weightlifting can both improve sleep quality. But timing matters: exercise in the morning or afternoon helps sleep, while exercise too close to bedtime can make it hard to fall asleep.

Card C
Sleep

Sleep gives the body and brain time to recover. Most adults need at least seven hours, but about one-third of Americans get less. Without enough sleep, people often eat more and choose high-calorie food, because sleep loss changes the hormones [body chemicals] that control hunger. Tired people also exercise less and get more sports injuries.

Ending: Which is most important? The article says we cannot choose one, because the three are so closely connected. If you don’t have time to work on all three, a doctor can give you personal advice.
